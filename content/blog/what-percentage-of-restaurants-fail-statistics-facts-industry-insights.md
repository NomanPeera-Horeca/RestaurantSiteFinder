Restaurant failure is a real business risk, but the familiar claim that 90% fail in their first year is not supported by credible research. Historical restaurant studies have reported first-year failure rates of approximately 17% to 26%, depending on the population and definition used. Understanding those differences—and the operating decisions behind them—is more useful than treating any single percentage as your restaurant’s destiny.

## What percentage of restaurants fail in the first year?

Two widely cited restaurant studies found first-year failure rates of 17% and approximately 26%, not 90%. Tian Luo and Philip B. Stark analyzed independently owned, full-service restaurant startups in the western United States and reported 17%. H. G. Parsa and colleagues’ research using Columbus, Ohio, data from 1996–1999 reported approximately 26%. These are historical findings from different populations, not a current nationwide forecast.

For readers comparing restaurant failure rate first year statistics, the essential details belong beside the percentage:

| Research source | Population and period | First-year finding | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Luo and Stark, research released in 2014 | Independently owned, full-service startups in the western U.S., using a 20-year dataset | 17% failed | Not every restaurant format or U.S. market |
| Parsa and colleagues, published in 2005 | Columbus, Ohio, restaurant research using 1996–1999 data | Approximately 26% failed | Local, historical research involving ownership turnover |

Both studies challenge the 90% myth, but neither establishes a universal probability for a restaurant opening today.

Use these findings to frame risk, not to calculate your opening budget. Before relying on a statistic, ask whether its geography, restaurant format, and definition resemble your planned business. A figure without that context offers little help when choosing between two sites or deciding how much operating cash to retain.

## Reading three- and five-year statistics correctly

Longer-term figures require the same care. Ohio State’s account of Parsa’s Columbus research reported a cumulative failure rate of approximately 59% over three years. That finding describes one historical market; it is not evidence that 59% of all U.S. restaurants close within three years today.

Federal data provides a broader comparison. Among accommodation and food services establishments born in 2013, Bureau of Labor Statistics figures show 82.5% surviving one year, 65.6% surviving three years, and 55.3% surviving five years. The corresponding shares no longer operating were therefore 17.5%, 34.4%, and 44.7%. This category includes accommodation businesses as well as food services, so it should not be labeled a restaurant-only failure rate.

For that same cohort, five-year survival across the entire private sector was 50.6%, below accommodation and food services’ 55.3%. This comparison challenges the assumption that restaurant-related businesses must always perform worse than other businesses. It does not establish whether the surviving establishments were profitable.

When reviewing restaurant business failure rate statistics, check four things:

- **The cohort:** Were businesses followed from opening, or were established businesses included?
- **The endpoint:** Does failure mean closure, bankruptcy, or ownership change?
- **The denominator:** Does an annual percentage describe original openings or only the businesses still operating?
- **The category:** Are the figures restaurant-specific or drawn from a broader industry?

Do not combine a three-year restaurant turnover rate with a five-year accommodation-sector closure rate to create a single survival curve. They measure different populations and outcomes. For a deeper walkthrough of definitions and BLS tables, see [restaurant failure rate statistics: what the data reveals](/blog/restaurant-failure-rate-statistics-what-data-reveals-industry).

## Closure, ownership turnover, and bankruptcy are different

Parsa and colleagues explicitly noted that restaurant failure lacks a universal definition. Their discussion distinguishes financial distress from strategic closures and managerial or personal reasons for leaving the business. Research based only on bankruptcies will consequently capture a narrower set of outcomes than research tracking ownership turnover.

For planning purposes, define your own success criteria too. Remaining open is one measure; paying the owner appropriately and operating without unsustainable working hours are others. Assess the business against all three rather than celebrating survival while ignoring its cost.

## Why do restaurants fail beyond food quality?

Restaurant failure can involve financial controls, concept execution, management capacity, and the owner’s personal circumstances—not simply whether customers enjoy the food. Parsa’s research examined these broader dimensions, including burnout and family-life pressures. The practical response is to evaluate the whole operating model, rather than looking for one defective menu item or marketing campaign.

### Budget for the ramp-up, not just opening day

Build two separate budgets: one for getting the doors open and another for operating while demand develops. Include deposits, equipment, preopening payroll, initial inventory, and contingency needs in the first. In the second, model the timing of customer receipts and recurring payments.

Stress-test the plan before committing. Lower expected customer counts, delay the opening, and increase selected expenses in separate scenarios. Then identify which decisions you could actually change: hiring pace, opening hours, purchasing quantities, or launch scope.

Avoid choosing a cash reserve simply because another operator recommended a round number. Base it on your projected shortfalls, payment commitments, and realistic downside scenarios. Review the assumptions with a qualified adviser before taking on financing or signing major commitments.

### Watch the dollars left after each sale

Thin margins make cost discipline consequential. The National Restaurant Association’s 2025 Operations Data Abstract, reporting 2024 results, found median income before taxes of 2.8% of sales for full-service respondents and 4.0% for limited-service respondents. Those are survey benchmarks, not promised returns or targets for every restaurant.

Create a weekly review covering ingredient purchases, waste, payroll, discounts, refunds, and channel-specific expenses. Compare actual spending with your own budget and investigate changes before assuming higher revenue will solve them. For each menu item, consider preparation time and ingredient overlap alongside selling price.

Labor deserves particular attention without becoming an excuse for indiscriminate cuts. Association survey respondents reporting full-service profits in 2024 had lower median labor-cost ratios than respondents reporting losses. That association is useful for diagnosis, but it does not prove that cutting staff creates profitability. See [what is prime cost—formula and benchmarks](/blog/what-is-prime-cost-restaurant-formula-examples-benchmarks) for a combined COGS and labor view.

### Give customers a clear reason to return

Write down your intended customer, primary dining occasion, expected spend, and reason someone would choose you over nearby alternatives. Then test whether the menu, service style, atmosphere, and operating hours support that promise. Ask prospective customers to describe the concept back to you; confusion is worth resolving before launch.

After opening, review repeat visits alongside new-customer acquisition. Where your systems and customer permissions allow, track returning guests and responses to specific offers. Use loyalty rewards selectively, with a clear spending limit and a reason to believe the offer encourages another profitable visit.

### Design a business the team can operate

Owner sustainability belongs in the plan. Ohio State’s reporting on the Columbus research described closures connected to health, retirement, family circumstances, and the time commitments of running a restaurant. Financial viability and willingness to continue operating were not always the same thing.

Assign responsibility for ordering, scheduling, cash checks, and service recovery. Document essential routines and train someone to cover each critical task. Hold brief manager check-ins so recurring problems reach the person who can fix them instead of becoming a permanent burden on the owner.

## Restaurant type and location need context

Historical comparisons suggest that business format matters, but they do not support a simple safe-versus-risky ranking. The Columbus research reported three-year failure rates of roughly 57% for franchised chains and 61% for independents. A franchise label was not immunity from failure in that sample.

Treat precise cuisine-level rankings cautiously. Before accepting a claim that one cuisine is dramatically riskier than another, request the sample size, location, study period, and definition of failure. A local ownership-turnover result should not become a national forecast for every operator serving that food.

Instead, compare the demands of your proposed formats. Estimate staffing by service period, equipment needs, preparation complexity, seating requirements, and realistic order volume. A smaller menu or footprint may simplify your plan, but test the numbers rather than assuming simplicity guarantees survival.

## Match the site to the customer occasion

Parsa’s study found a strong correlation between restaurant density and ownership turnover in its sample. That is a reason to investigate competition carefully, not proof that every busy restaurant district is a poor location. Correlation alone cannot establish why an individual business closed.

For a Restaurant Site Finder shortlist, use a consistent site-visit checklist:

- **Observe relevant hours:** Visit during the breakfast, lunch, or evening periods your concept needs.
- **Test access:** Walk the approach, inspect visibility, and experience parking or pickup as a customer would.
- **Map alternatives:** Include grocery prepared meals, cafés, and delivery options—not just matching cuisines.
- **Check the premises:** Investigate utility capacity, ventilation, layout, and required improvements with appropriate professionals.
- **Model total occupancy:** Include the proposed lease obligations and anticipated premises expenses, not base rent alone.

Keep the same assumptions across every property in your Restaurant Site Finder comparison. A beautiful dining room should not receive optimistic sales assumptions while a less attractive alternative receives conservative ones. Record unanswered questions and resolve them before choosing a favorite. ## Food industry trends change the survival playbook

Recent restaurant industry analysis points to continued pressure rather than effortless growth. The National Restaurant Association’s 2026 industry outlook highlights uneven traffic, rising costs, constrained household budgets, and investment in workforce development and technology. Sector-wide growth and individual restaurant profitability should therefore be evaluated separately.

Off-premises dining is one important adaptation. In the Association’s 2025 consumer research, 47% of adults reported picking up takeout at least weekly, while 37% reported ordering delivery at least weekly. These figures demonstrate customer habits, not the profitability of adding either channel to a particular restaurant.

Before expanding delivery, test a limited menu and calculate the contribution after ingredients, packaging, platform charges, and incremental labor. Check food quality after realistic travel times and watch whether extra orders disrupt dine-in service. Expand only after the trial meets your financial and service requirements.

Apply the same discipline to direct ordering, loyalty software, and kitchen technology. Define the problem first, choose a measurable outcome, and assign someone to review results. Technology should earn its place in the operation rather than becoming another subscription nobody evaluates.

## Turn the statistics into a practical operating review

Restaurant failure rate statistics are most useful when they prompt specific decisions. Instead of revisiting a national percentage every month, establish a short review that connects your assumptions to actual performance. Give each issue an owner and a date for follow-up.

- **Update cash expectations:** Compare upcoming commitments with available funds and realistic receipts.
- **Separate traffic from spending:** Check guest counts and average checks independently.
- **Investigate cost changes:** Look at waste, purchasing, staffing, and discounts before selecting a remedy.
- **Review the guest experience:** Read complaints for repeated operational problems, not just isolated criticism.
- **Choose one test:** Adjust a service period, menu item, or offer, then evaluate the result before widening it.

The takeaway is not that restaurants are safe—or doomed. Start with properly labeled evidence, then build a plan around your customers, site, cash needs, and team capacity. Your most useful next step is to challenge one fragile assumption before it becomes an expensive commitment.
