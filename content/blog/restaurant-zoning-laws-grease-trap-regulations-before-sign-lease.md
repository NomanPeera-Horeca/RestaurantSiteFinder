Before signing a restaurant lease, verify that your proposed operation is allowed at the address, identify the approvals needed to occupy and operate the space, and confirm that its grease-control system meets local requirements. A previous restaurant tenant does not automatically establish approval for your concept. This guide explains how to investigate restaurant zoning laws and grease trap regulations before committing to a location, with local examples that illustrate why verification matters.

## What restaurant zoning requirements should you verify before signing?

Verify the property's zoning district, its established permitted use, and whether your specific restaurant concept needs additional approval. Restaurant zoning requirements depend on the location and proposed operation, not simply whether a listing describes the space as commercial. Major cities advise businesses to check zoning before committing to a location and warn against assuming that an existing restaurant provides automatic use approval.

Start with the planning department's property map and permit records, then ask staff to evaluate your actual concept. Describe the menu, cooking methods, seating, alcohol service, hours, outdoor dining, and any entertainment. These details help distinguish your proposal from the previous tenant's operation.

Food-business guidance in many jurisdictions distinguishes restaurants, drinking establishments, and catering uses, and may restrict outdoor seating. The practical lesson is to ask about your business model rather than requesting a general confirmation that “restaurants are allowed.”

Use this checklist when discussing local zoning codes:

- **Use classification:** Ask which classification applies to the complete operation, including production or catering activities.
- **Approval pathway:** Confirm whether the use is permitted outright or needs a conditional-use or other discretionary approval.
- **Site restrictions:** Ask about applicable parking, outdoor seating, signage, loading, and operating restrictions.
- **Existing approvals:** Request the conditions attached to earlier approvals and ask whether they cover your proposal.
- **Written confirmation:** Keep the department's response, underlying records, and any remaining questions together.

Treat urban planning laws and land use laws as address-specific research. Avoid assuming that approval at a nearby property proves zoning compliance at yours.

## Zoning approval is only one part of restaurant licensing

Zoning addresses whether a use belongs at a location; it does not replace all other restaurant permits. Health, building, plumbing, fire, and occupancy approvals can involve separate reviews. Planning, building-code, fire-marshal, and other authorities may each require action before restaurant licensing.

Build a permit matrix instead of relying on one broad question about whether the space is “licensed.” For each agency, record the application, required documents, responsible professional, review stage, and approval needed before the next task can proceed.

Ask the relevant departments to identify which of these apply:

| Approval area | What to establish before committing |
| --- | --- |
| Planning and zoning | Whether the proposed use and site activities are allowed |
| Building and occupancy | Whether the approved use and proposed alterations require further review |
| Health department | Whether the menu, layout, equipment, and operating plan need plan review |
| Plumbing and wastewater | Whether drainage and grease-control arrangements are acceptable |
| Fire and mechanical systems | Which cooking, ventilation, and fire-safety reviews are necessary |
| Additional activities | Whether alcohol, signs, entertainment, or outdoor dining need separate authorization |

Do not confuse a food-service permit with permission to use the building. Health permits typically do not cover all city and state requirements and must be read alongside the certificate of occupancy. See our [restaurant permits and licenses guide](/blog/restaurant-permits-licenses-guide) for how layers fit together.

## Investigate the space's approved history

Ask the landlord for the occupancy documentation, approved plans, permit history, inspection records, and notices relating to unresolved conditions. Compare those records with the existing layout and your proposed alterations. Certificates of occupancy and permitting history help establish a property's approved use.

When researching restaurant occupancy permits, use the terminology your building department recognizes. Ask which document establishes lawful occupancy, whether your proposed use matches it, and whether any new approval is necessary. Have your architect or other qualified professional help interpret discrepancies rather than treating the landlord's description as a determination.

Pay particular attention to changes in layout and operations. Plan reviewers may need your menu, scaled floor plan, plumbing fixtures, seating, ventilation, hot water, and garbage storage—not an equipment list alone.

Recommend a document review and a site walkthrough before finalizing your improvement budget. Mark anything that cannot yet be verified as an open item. An unresolved item should remain visible in your decision process, not disappear into a general renovation allowance. ## Does the existing grease trap meet your kitchen's requirements?

An existing grease trap is not proof that the proposed kitchen has an acceptable grease-control system. Confirm its approved type, sizing, connected fixtures, condition, accessibility, and suitability with the plumbing and wastewater authorities. Environmental and sewer agencies require properly sized and designed interceptors; ownership and occupancy changes may trigger new connection requirements.

Grease interceptors separate fats, oils, and grease—often called FOG—from kitchen wastewater before it enters the sewer. They need regular maintenance to continue working properly. Authorities distinguish hydromechanical interceptors (sometimes called grease traps) from larger gravity interceptors, which is why equipment specifications matter more than informal names.

### Verify sizing and fixture connections

Ask a qualified plumbing professional to inspect the system and document its manufacturer, model, rating, location, and connected fixtures. Provide your proposed menu, equipment schedule, and expected operating volume. Request a written explanation of any assumptions that still need agency confirmation.

Wastewater authorities may request kitchen wastewater plans, fixture connections, interceptor specifications, tenant improvements, the menu, and estimated meals per day—plus maintenance access. Use those categories to prepare your questions, not as a substitute for the rules at your address.

Searches for “grease trap commercial kitchen code” should lead to your local plumbing authority and sewer provider, not a universal sizing recommendation. Ask which sizing method and equipment standards they accept. Confirm the fixture schedule before purchasing equipment or approving the final kitchen layout. Pair grease review with [hood system](/glossary/hood-system) and ventilation planning for the full mechanical picture.

### Check access and installation feasibility

Request a walkthrough that includes the interceptor lid, service route, drainage connections, and any proposed replacement location. Ask the contractor to identify work involving floors, exterior areas, or shared property. Then ask the landlord for written permission covering the specific work being considered.

Many programs require grease interceptors in an approved location that is safely accessible for inspection, cleaning, and waste removal—and address installation sequencing before final permit inspections. Access and approval timing belong in the initial feasibility review rather than the finishing stages.

### Ownership changes and maintenance deserve separate checks

Do not assume that keeping the same equipment means keeping the same approval status. FOG programs often address new construction, redevelopment, tenant improvements, and changes in ownership or occupancy. Licensing agencies may require notice of menu or ownership changes as well.

Ask each relevant agency whether your takeover, remodel, or operational changes require an application, inspection, or updated documentation. Explain what will remain unchanged as well as what will change. Request confirmation before relying on any claim that an installation is “grandfathered.”

Maintenance also belongs in your operating budget. Cleaning frequency may depend on establishment type, interceptor size, and discharged flow, with reporting after each service. Do not assume an enzyme product replaces required maintenance.

Before signing, request recent service records and ask potential service providers about access, cleaning scope, reporting, and disposal arrangements. Separately plan staff practices such as scraping food debris and keeping grease out of drains.

## How can the lease address unresolved approval risks?

Ask a local commercial real estate attorney to help negotiate language addressing approval deadlines, access for investigations, responsibility for improvements, and options if the proposed restaurant cannot obtain necessary approvals. Treat the following as negotiation topics, not ready-to-use legal clauses. The appropriate terms depend on the property, proposed work, and negotiated agreement.

Bring the attorney a written list of unresolved zoning, occupancy, plumbing, and wastewater questions. Include preliminary agency responses and contractor findings. That gives the discussion a concrete scope instead of relying on a general promise that the premises are suitable.

Consider requesting provisions addressing:

- **Due-diligence access:** Permission for professional inspections and agreed testing before deadlines expire.
- **Landlord cooperation:** Access to records, signatures, and authorization for permit submissions.
- **Improvement responsibility:** A clear allocation of investigation, design, installation, repair, and restoration costs.
- **Shared equipment:** Defined service access, maintenance responsibilities, recordkeeping, and cost allocation for shared interceptors.
- **Approval milestones:** Dates tied to clearly identified approvals rather than an undefined expectation of being ready to open.
- **Unsuccessful approvals:** Attorney-drafted terms addressing termination, deposits, and other consequences if specified approvals cannot be obtained.

Review the proposed lease alongside the construction scope and budget. If the contractor assumes the landlord provides access or performs work, ask counsel to reconcile that assumption with the agreement. Do not leave a critical obligation resting only in an email or conversation. ## Turn due diligence into a clear signing decision

Create one decision file containing your concept description, property records, agency correspondence, preliminary plans, professional findings, and proposed lease terms. Organize it by unresolved question rather than by the order documents arrived. For each issue, identify who must answer it and what evidence will close it.

Use a simple decision checklist:

- **Confirm the concept:** Give every reviewer the same menu, seating plan, equipment assumptions, and operating description.
- **Verify the address:** Check the use classification, applicable zoning regulations, approved history, and additional approval pathway.
- **Inspect the infrastructure:** Obtain professional findings on drainage, grease control, and proposed alterations.
- **Price the defined work:** Request estimates that identify exclusions, assumptions, and items awaiting agency decisions.
- **Resolve the agreement:** Ask counsel to align access, responsibilities, deadlines, and approval risks with the negotiated lease.

Before making the final decision, distinguish confirmed facts from optimistic assumptions. A preliminary conversation is useful, but ask what formal review remains necessary. Keep enough flexibility in your plan to respond to information that emerges during that review. If critical gates fail, use a [go/no-go restaurant location decision](/blog/go-no-go-restaurant-location-decision) before you waive contingencies.

## The takeaway before you commit

Make the signing decision about a verified location and a defined project—not simply an attractive dining room. Prioritize confirmation of the permitted use, the remaining approval process, grease-system suitability, and who will perform and pay for necessary work. When those answers remain incomplete, bring the open questions to your local reviewers, qualified professionals, and attorney before making an unconditional commitment.
