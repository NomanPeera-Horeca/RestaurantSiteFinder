Restaurant failure rates are substantially lower than the familiar claim that 90% close during their first year. Research on independent full-service restaurants found approximately 17% failed in year one, while federal data for the broader accommodation and food services sector show 55.3% of establishments born in 2013 remained open five years later. These benchmarks offer a more realistic starting point for assessing risk—but only when you understand what each dataset measures.

For prospective owners, the useful question is not simply whether restaurants survive. It is how to translate restaurant industry data into better decisions about location, capital, staffing, and daily management.

## What percentage of restaurants fail in their first year?

There is no single first-year restaurant failure rate that applies to every concept and market. Tian Luo and Philip B. Stark’s research found that approximately 17% of independently owned full-service restaurant startups in the western United States failed within their first year, compared with 19% of other service-business startups. Their analysis covered approximately 81,000 restaurants using two decades of establishment records.

An earlier Columbus, Ohio, study reported approximately 26% first-year restaurant ownership turnover. Published in 2005, that research tracked restaurants during 1996–1999 and counted ownership changes, making its results different from a straightforward measure of permanent closures. Neither study supports the 90% claim.

The practical takeaway: credible historical studies suggest a much less catastrophic first year than the myth implies. However, those studies are not forecasts for a restaurant opening today. Their geography, observation periods, business definitions, and measurement methods matter.

When evaluating restaurant failure rate first-year statistics, keep the source attached to the number. A percentage without a defined population or time frame offers little guidance for an investment decision. ## Reading the one-, five-, and ten-year benchmarks

The Bureau of Labor Statistics provides a useful longer-term comparison by following establishments born in the same year. For accommodation and food services establishments born in 2013, the following shares remained operating at successive milestones. This category includes accommodation businesses as well as food services, so it is not a restaurant-only dataset.

| Time since opening | Still operating | No longer operating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One year, March 2014 | 82.5% | 17.5% |
| Three years, March 2016 | 65.6% | 34.4% |
| Five years, March 2018 | 55.3% | 44.7% |
| Ten years, March 2023 | 38.2% | 61.8% |

The survival figures come from BLS; the final column is calculated by subtracting survival from 100%. These figures describe one opening cohort, not universal restaurant success rates.

At five years, that sector’s survival rate exceeded the 50.6% rate for all private-sector establishments in the same cohort. At ten years, it also exceeded the overall private-sector rate of 34.7%. The comparison challenges the assumption that hospitality businesses inevitably perform worse than businesses generally.

Treat these figures as perspective, not a personal probability. A national sector average cannot account for your lease, financing, experience, customer base, or operating model.

## The definitions that change the story

Before comparing restaurant business failure rate statistics, establish what “failure” actually means. The Cornell study discusses the lack of a universal definition, while Ohio State’s account explains that bankruptcy measures exclude restaurants closing for nonfinancial reasons. Owners may leave because of health, retirement, or family pressures rather than an economically unworkable business.

Three distinctions deserve particular attention:

- **Closure versus bankruptcy:** ending operations is broader than a formal bankruptcy filing. Neither measure alone explains the owner’s financial outcome.
- **Ownership turnover versus disappearance:** a restaurant can change hands without its dining room permanently closing.
- **First-year failure versus annual closures:** newly opened restaurants are a different population from all restaurants operating during a calendar year.

The denominator matters just as much. Ten closures among 100 establishments represent a different rate from ten among 1,000. Similarly, tracking only businesses already established at the start of a year does not reveal how many brand-new restaurants survive their first twelve months.

Before saving a statistic to your business plan, record its publication date, observation period, geography, business category, and definition of failure. If those details are missing, look for stronger evidence rather than treating the headline as a benchmark.

## Post-pandemic improvements need careful interpretation

Datassential reported 886 U.S. restaurant closures in April 2025, compared with 1,645 in February and 1,655 in March. It described April’s preliminary count as at least a seven-year low. That is evidence of fewer recorded closures in a particular month—not a first-year survival rate for newly opened restaurants.

Pandemic-era comparisons also require care. The BLS cohort above reached its five-year milestone in 2018, before COVID-19, but its ten-year result includes the pandemic period. Comparing those milestones does not isolate the pandemic’s effect; doing that requires an appropriate comparison group and additional analysis.

Lower closure counts should not be confused with easy operating conditions. The National Restaurant Association’s 2025 report identified elevated food and labor costs, alongside insurance, utilities, and payment-processing costs, as significant operator challenges. Business continuity and financial comfort are different outcomes.

Use recent food service statistics to understand the trading environment, but use cohort survival research to assess business longevity. Combining unrelated measures can produce reassuring—or alarming—conclusions that the underlying evidence does not support.

## Segment and cuisine comparisons require context

Restaurant format can be useful context, but closure counts alone cannot establish which concept is safest. In Datassential’s April 2025 analysis, quick-service restaurants recorded the most closures and the most openings. Fine dining recorded the fewest closures, but it also represented the smallest segment by unit count.

Cuisine comparisons have the same limitation. American, Mexican, and coffee/bakery categories accounted for the most closures in that analysis, yet they also led opening counts. Without comparable rates and category sizes, these counts do not establish that one cuisine offers better survival odds.

For your own research, prioritize comparisons with restaurants serving similar occasions, price points, and neighborhoods. A weekday lunch concept should be tested against weekday demand, not merely against the national popularity of its cuisine. Likewise, examine whether nearby closures shared an access problem, an unsuitable space, or a customer occasion your concept would also depend on.

## Why do restaurants fail even when customers like the food?

Restaurants can struggle because food quality is only one part of a viable business. The Cornell research identified management capabilities, financial management, sufficient capital, operational standards, concept clarity, location, and staff training among the factors relevant to success or failure. It does not support reducing every closure to a single cause.

Turn those findings into practical tests rather than a generic list of risks.

### Cash needs must extend beyond opening day

Separate your opening budget from your operating forecast. Include deposits, equipment, and preopening expenses in the first; then model payroll, purchasing, occupancy, maintenance, and cash collections in the second. Test a slower sales ramp rather than assuming the restaurant reaches its target immediately.

For example, build a downside scenario in which weekday demand develops more slowly than weekend demand. Identify which bills remain fixed and decide in advance what would trigger a revised schedule, smaller purchasing commitments, or additional funding. This is a planning exercise, not a prediction of how your restaurant will perform.

When considering locations through Restaurant Site Finder, start with the customer occasion rather than the attractiveness of the space. A useful location checklist includes:

- **Demand timing:** observe the area during the meal periods you intend to serve.
- **Customer access:** walk the route from parking, transit, or neighboring destinations.
- **Concept fit:** test whether the intended price point and service style match the occasion.
- **Space suitability:** investigate layout, utilities, equipment needs, and required professional assessments.
- **Downside affordability:** model occupancy expenses against conservative sales, not only your target case.

### Management needs repeatable routines

Write down opening, closing, ordering, recipe, service-recovery, and shift-handover procedures. Assign responsibility for reviewing exceptions, such as unusual waste or repeated stockouts. Set a review schedule so problems lead to a decision rather than another week of improvisation.

Keep marketing equally testable. Define the customer occasion, explain the offer clearly, and evaluate whether promotions attract repeat visits at an acceptable contribution. Avoid treating a busy promotional night as proof that the underlying business model works.

### Track operating signals, not just sales

Industry benchmarks can help identify questions, but they should not become automatic targets. Among respondents to the National Restaurant Association’s 2025 operating survey, median labor costs including benefits represented 36.5% of full-service sales and 31.7% of limited-service sales in 2024. The Association explicitly cautions that its figures are management tools, not standards for individual restaurants.

Build a compact weekly dashboard around your own forecast:

| Measure | What to review | Possible next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash forecast | Expected receipts and payment dates | Adjust commitments before a shortfall |
| Food cost percentage | Ingredient usage relative to food sales | Check yields, portions, waste, and pricing |
| Labor cost percentage | Labor spending relative to sales | Review scheduling alongside service quality |
| Guest count and average check | Whether changes reflect visits or spending | Investigate traffic and menu mix separately |
| Item contribution | Revenue remaining after relevant variable costs | Reassess pricing, promotion, or menu placement |

Use consistent definitions so comparisons remain meaningful. For instance, decide which benefits belong in labor costs and which expenses your item-contribution calculation includes. Record the reason for each corrective action, then review whether it produced the intended result.

## Can training improve a restaurant’s chances of survival?

Training can address capabilities linked to restaurant performance, including financial management, staff development, operational consistency, and leadership. However, the cited studies do not establish a universal percentage improvement in survival from earning a culinary or business qualification. Treat education as preparation for specific responsibilities, not insurance against closure.

Choose learning opportunities around practical outputs. A financial course should help you build and interpret a cash forecast. Operations training should help you document recipes, purchasing controls, and service standards. Leadership development should prepare you to delegate, coach, and handle difficult conversations.

Combine instruction with realistic practice where possible. Ask an experienced operator to challenge your assumptions, rehearse a shift handover, and explain your cost model without relying on optimistic sales. The aim is to identify gaps while they are still inexpensive to address.

## Turn the statistics into a stronger plan

Restaurant survival research offers a more balanced picture than the 90% first-year failure myth, but no national statistic can validate an individual concept. Historical studies and broader federal benchmarks are useful precisely when their limitations remain visible.

Use the evidence to ask sharper questions: Does the location support the customer occasion? Can the business withstand slower sales? Who owns the weekly financial review?
