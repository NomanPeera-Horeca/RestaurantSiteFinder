Choosing a restaurant location means testing whether a specific property can attract your customers, support your operation, and work financially before you commit. This 10-step feasibility checklist gives you a practical framework for comparing sites, gathering evidence, and identifying deal breakers. Use it to build a restaurant feasibility study—not simply a shortlist of attractive storefronts.

## 1. What is the best location for a restaurant?

The best location for a restaurant is one that fits its intended customers, service model, operating needs, and financial limits. Rather than looking for a universally perfect neighborhood, start with your concept and evaluate each address against it. The Small Business Administration recommends considering target markets, location-specific expenses, and local restrictions when choosing business premises.

Write a one-page location brief before arranging tours. Define:

- **Customer:** Who you expect to serve and what brings them out.
- **Occasion:** Quick lunch, family dinner, date night, drinks, or takeout.
- **Price position:** Your planned average check and value proposition.
- **Service model:** Counter service, table service, delivery, or a combination.
- **Nonnegotiables:** Required kitchen capabilities, opening hours, and access.

For example, evaluate a lunch-focused counter-service concept around convenient weekday visits; evaluate a dinner-led concept around evening demand. These are hypotheses to test, not guarantees. Keep the brief visible during every tour so attractive finishes do not replace your original business requirements.

## 2. Define the trade area and investigate customer fit

If you are researching how to choose the best location for a new restaurant, start with the surrounding customer base—not the available properties. Use Census Business Builder to examine demographic and economic information for candidate areas. Its maps and downloadable reports support comparisons by location and business type.

Build a profile of the people who live, work, study, or visit nearby. Compare household characteristics and local businesses with your proposed menu, pricing, and meal periods. Census data can help investigate customers and competition, but supplement it with direct observation rather than treating area-level figures as proof of demand.

Draw a realistic trade area around each property. Walk or drive the routes customers would use, noting crossings, disconnected streets, and inconvenient approaches. Record why someone would choose your restaurant from that area and what might stop them. [Trade area analysis for restaurants](/blog/trade-area-analysis-restaurants) expands boundary drawing and demand sources.

**Checkpoint:** Keep sites where you can explain the customer, occasion, and journey clearly. Flag assumptions that still need interviews or fieldwork.

## 3. Observe demand during your actual trading hours

Make timed observation part of your restaurant site selection process. Visit each finalist during the periods you intend to trade, using the same observation window so comparisons remain useful. Include ordinary weekdays and weekends rather than relying only on a lively event night.

Use a simple field sheet:

| Observation | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Passing activity | Pedestrians and vehicles during a defined interval |
| Relevant customers | People whose activities match your intended dining occasion |
| Nearby dining | Occupied tables, queues, and takeaway collections |
| Arrival conditions | Parking availability, crossings, and entrance visibility |
| Unusual factors | Weather, events, holidays, or construction |

Keep raw counts separate from interpretation. “Thirty pedestrians passed” is an observation; “they will buy lunch here” is an assumption. Repeat visits where results vary sharply.

Ask nearby operators about quiet periods, but treat their comments as leads to investigate. The goal is a defensible picture of normal trading conditions, including the hours when the street feels least promising. For foot-traffic nuance, see [high foot traffic vs high restaurant sales](/blog/high-foot-traffic-vs-high-restaurant-sales-which-location-better).

## 4. Map competitors and identify a specific opening

Use competitor research to sharpen your proposition rather than simply count restaurants. The Census Bureau provides business data that can help investigate existing establishments; combine that starting point with visits to individual competitors.

Map direct alternatives alongside businesses serving the same occasion. For a quick lunch concept, include grocery prepared foods and sandwich shops—not just restaurants offering similar cuisine. Record menu positioning, opening hours, ordering options, and the experience you observe.

If you are considering the best location for a Mexican grill restaurant, test the specific format. A fast-casual grill needs a different location brief from a full-service restaurant built around leisurely dinners and drinks. Avoid assuming that neighborhood demographics alone establish demand for a cuisine.

Write one sentence explaining the opening: “We plan to serve this customer, during this meal period, with this distinct offer.” Then identify the evidence supporting it. If the difference rests entirely on being cheaper, revisit your cost assumptions before proceeding. Follow the worksheet approach in [how to analyze restaurant competition before choosing a location](/blog/analyze-restaurant-competition-before-choosing-location).

## 5. Test visibility, arrival, and accessible entry

Walk through the complete arrival experience at each site. Approach from different directions, find the entrance without instructions, and test the route from transit stops or parking areas. Ask what a first-time guest would need to notice or navigate.

For U.S. properties, include accessibility in the assessment rather than postponing it until design. Department of Justice guidance addresses access to entrances, dining areas, service counters, and restrooms, with obligations depending on the facility and circumstances. Have a qualified professional assess the property’s requirements.

Add these practical checks to your tour:

- Identify proposed sign positions and request confirmation that they are permitted.
- Check parking rights rather than assuming nearby spaces are available to customers.
- Trace an accessible journey into and through the premises.
- Locate pickup waiting areas without blocking dining access.
- Revisit after dark to assess lighting and the arrival experience.

**Checkpoint:** Document the improvements needed, who must approve them, and their estimated costs before comparing this site with easier alternatives.

## 6. Can the location support your projected sales and costs?

A location passes the financial test only when your documented sales assumptions can cover its operating costs, with an opening cash plan you can fund. Build a site-specific model rather than applying a universal rent percentage. Location-dependent expenses can include rent, wages, insurance, utilities, taxes, and government fees.

Start with transparent sales calculations. For dine-in service, estimate seats, realistic seat turns, average spend, and trading days. Model takeaway separately so you do not count the same customers twice. Label every input as observed, quoted, or assumed.

For a basic operating break-even calculation:

**Break-even monthly sales = monthly fixed operating costs ÷ contribution margin ratio.**

The contribution margin ratio is the share of revenue remaining after variable costs. Separate fixed and variable portions of mixed expenses, including labor, as appropriate.

Run a downside case with fewer customers, higher costs, and a slower opening ramp. Separately account for startup spending, financing payments, and cash reserves; operating break-even alone is not a complete cash-flow test. See [restaurant market feasibility study](/blog/restaurant-market-feasibility-study-evaluate-location-before-investing) and [how to know if a restaurant location will be profitable](/blog/how-to-know-restaurant-location-will-be-profitable).

## 7. Inspect the building against the menu and workflow

Bring your proposed menu and equipment list to a technical inspection. Ask qualified restaurant design and construction professionals to assess what the space can support and what alterations need investigation. Verify the applicable building, fire, and health requirements locally.

Request written findings on ventilation, electrical capacity, plumbing, grease management, refrigeration, drainage, restrooms, and equipment condition. Distinguish equipment that is present from equipment confirmed suitable for your operation. Ask for upgrade estimates and identify unresolved conditions before treating a former restaurant as ready to reopen.

Then sketch movement through receiving, storage, preparation, cooking, service, and washing. Include staff, guests, and pickup drivers.

When evaluating the best location for a bar in a restaurant, compare layouts that allow guests to order comfortably without obstructing entrances, accessible routes, or server circulation. Treat bar placement as an operational design decision, not just a visual centerpiece.

## 8. Verify permitted use and operating approvals

Confirm that your intended operation is allowed before making an unconditional commitment. Zoning restrictions are generally administered locally, and the SBA advises checking with the relevant planning department. A property’s availability for lease is not confirmation that your concept is permitted there.

Prepare a written description of your proposed restaurant, including cooking methods, service hours, alcohol plans, outdoor seating, entertainment, and takeaway activity. Ask the relevant local authorities which approvals apply to that exact proposal. Also request available records for the building’s approved use and prior alterations.

Keep an approvals register showing:

- The authority or professional responsible for each determination.
- Required applications, supporting drawings, and inspections.
- Known fees and estimated timelines, with their sources.
- Conditions that could prevent your intended operation.

Apply the same verification mindset locally rather than assuming another jurisdiction’s process matches yours. Cross-check against the [25-point site selection checklist before you sign a lease](/blog/restaurant-site-selection-checklist-25-things-before-lease).

## 9. Investigate staffing, deliveries, and everyday logistics

Expand your restaurant site selection criteria beyond the guest-facing space. Business-location guidance recommends investigating access to qualified employees, infrastructure, and services.

Conduct an operational walk-through with your planned opening and closing routines in mind. Test employee travel options at actual shift times, and ask suppliers how they would deliver to the premises. Locate refuse storage, collection points, and any shared service corridors.

Use practical scenarios: a supplier arrives during lunch, a delivery driver waits while the entrance is busy, or closing staff leave after transit service becomes limited. Record how each situation would be handled rather than assuming the team will improvise.

Check internet availability and installation permissions for your ordering and payment setup. Request written clarification of shared access rights and service restrictions. Include necessary fixes in the financial model, then reconsider whether the property still fits your operating brief.

## 10. Compare finalists and review the lease before committing

Finish with a side-by-side scorecard, not a memory of your favorite tour. Use the same evidence categories for every property and attach supporting notes. Treat the following as a suggested decision tool, not an industry-standard weighting system:

| Category | Evidence to attach |
| --- | --- |
| Customer fit | Area research and customer observations |
| Demand | Repeated counts across intended trading periods |
| Access | Arrival tests and accessibility assessment |
| Economics | Quotes, sales assumptions, and downside model |
| Building suitability | Technical inspection and upgrade estimates |
| Approvals | Written findings and unresolved applications |
| Operational fit | Staffing, supplier, and service-access checks |

Separate deal breakers from scored preferences. Do not let attractive frontage compensate for an unresolved approval or an unaffordable conversion.

Have a qualified local attorney review the proposed lease. Request clear treatment of total occupancy charges, repair responsibilities, permitted use, alteration rights, guarantees, and any negotiated contingencies. Business-location guidance emphasizes investigating costs beyond base rent, including shared charges and build-out expenses.

Mark each site proceed, investigate, or reject, with reasons and outstanding actions. Do not convert an unanswered question into a favorable score.

## Choose the site you can justify

Learning how to choose a restaurant location is ultimately about replacing enthusiasm with evidence. These restaurant location tips give you a repeatable process: define the concept, investigate customers, observe demand, test the building, and model the commitment.

Keep your location brief, field notes, professional assessments, cost estimates, and approvals together. Choose the property whose strengths survive scrutiny—and be prepared to walk away when an unresolved risk undermines the plan.
