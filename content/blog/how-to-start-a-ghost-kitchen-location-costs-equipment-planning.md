A ghost kitchen prepares food for delivery or pickup without operating a traditional dining room. Starting one means matching a delivery-friendly menu with a suitable commercial kitchen, a realistic budget, approved equipment, and dependable order handling. This guide explains how to start a ghost kitchen by connecting location, costs, requirements, equipment, and layout into one practical launch plan.

## How much does it cost to start a ghost kitchen?

Ghost kitchen startup costs depend primarily on whether you rent an equipped kitchen, adapt an existing restaurant, or build a dedicated facility. Published estimates from industry sources often put a shared commissary launch notably below a private build-out. Treat these as directional vendor estimates—not quotes, guaranteed totals, or evidence that every location fits those ranges.

The important distinction is what your investment actually buys. An equipped rental may reduce construction spending while introducing storage charges, access restrictions, or service fees. A private space gives you more control, but you must establish which improvements and equipment are included before comparing prices.

### Build a budget around written quotes

Use the following categories as a quote checklist. Separate one-time expenses from monthly expenses rather than treating the opening budget as a single number.

| Budget category | What to confirm before committing |
| --- | --- |
| Space and deposits | Deposit, advance rent, storage, access hours, utilities, and shared-service charges |
| Construction and installation | Plumbing, electrical capacity, ventilation, fire protection, and contractor scope |
| Equipment and smallwares | Included appliances, missing equipment, freight, installation, and repairs |
| Approvals and protection | Permit fees, professional reviews, insurance quotes, and renewal dates |
| Opening operations | Ingredients, packaging, training, photography, ordering tools, and launch marketing |
| Working capital | Cash needed while sales develop, plus contingency for delays |

Request an itemized schedule of charges from every kitchen provider. Then build a monthly cash-flow forecast with conservative sales assumptions, including a delayed opening and slower-than-expected demand. Decide how much cash must remain available after deposits and equipment purchases rather than spending everything before the first order.

### Ghost kitchen vs. traditional restaurant overhead costs

A ghost kitchen can avoid dining-room construction, guest furniture, and dedicated table-service staffing. However, kitchen labor, utilities, sanitation, insurance, and food production remain. Lower startup spending does not automatically establish stronger profitability; compare both concepts using actual operating assumptions. ## How do you choose a ghost kitchen location?

Choose a location by testing customer access, kitchen suitability, and total occupancy cost—not street visibility alone. Start with the customers and meal occasions you want to serve, then investigate nearby commercial kitchens. Before signing, confirm that the proposed use is permitted and that the property can support your operation; zoning and location-specific expenses can materially affect the decision.

For your shortlist, investigate:

- **Delivery routes:** Test journeys to likely customers during the lunch or dinner periods you intend to serve. Record actual travel conditions rather than relying only on mileage.
- **Courier access:** Walk the route from parking to collection. Check entrances, building access, pickup instructions, and space for waiting drivers.
- **Production capacity:** Ask to see available refrigeration, dry storage, preparation space, loading access, and waste facilities.
- **Lease responsibility:** Establish who pays for repairs, equipment failure, pest control, cleaning, and required upgrades.

### Shared kitchen, dedicated rental, or private build-out

A shared commissary is worth investigating when you want to test demand without taking responsibility for an entire facility. Ask how workstations and equipment are booked, whether storage is allocated, and what happens when another operator needs the same appliance. Do not assume the facility’s existing approvals automatically cover your business; confirm your obligations with the permitting authority.

A dedicated rental deserves closer inspection when scheduling control matters. Request a written inventory and condition report for every included appliance. A private build-out should begin with a feasibility review, contractor estimates, and regulatory guidance—not an assumption that an inexpensive empty unit will become an inexpensive kitchen.

## Validate the menu before buying equipment

Make menu testing an early planning task. Prepare each proposed item, package it, and evaluate it after a journey resembling your intended delivery service. Note texture, temperature, leakage, appearance, and how easily a customer can finish or assemble the meal.

For example, test sauces packed separately from crisp components rather than assuming the dine-in presentation will travel well. Compare packaging options alongside portion costs. An item should earn its place through both customer appeal and workable preparation requirements.

For a multi-brand operation, map every concept against the same production schedule. Look for opportunities to share ingredients and equipment, but test the busiest combination of orders before adding another brand. Use the trial to identify whether cooking, assembly, packing, or collection becomes the constraint.

## What equipment does a ghost kitchen need?

A ghost kitchen needs equipment suited to its menu, production volume, and locally approved food-handling processes—not a universal shopping list. Confirm equipment acceptability before purchasing. Local authorities determine the requirements that apply to your establishment.

Build your proposed equipment schedule by task:

- **Storage:** Refrigerators, freezers, shelving, and food-safe containers sized for deliveries and preparation.
- **Preparation:** Worktables, scales, knives, cutting boards, and menu-specific preparation tools.
- **Cooking:** Only the ovens, ranges, fryers, griddles, or other appliances your tested menu requires.
- **Holding and packing:** Appropriate holding equipment, a packing bench, labels, bags, containers, and checking space.
- **Cleaning and monitoring:** Approved handwashing and warewashing facilities, sanitation supplies, and suitable thermometers.

Submit the menu and equipment schedule together during planning. Ask whether a commercial dishwasher is needed or an approved manual warewashing arrangement is suitable; do not decide from a generic equipment checklist. Your layout and installation must also leave equipment accessible for cleaning and maintenance. ### Buy used selectively and postpone nonessential purchases

Used equipment deserves inspection, not an automatic rejection or automatic purchase. Condition, certification, and local acceptance matter more than a seller’s claim that an appliance is “commercial.”

Before buying, request model information, service history, utility requirements, warranty terms, and a technician’s assessment where appropriate. Compare the installed cost—including transport and repairs—with a new alternative. Postpone display equipment, specialty appliances for untested dishes, and duplicate cooking equipment until you have a documented operational need.

## Design the layout around production and pickup

Sketch the intended workflow before placing equipment: receiving → storage → preparation → cooking → assembly → packing → handoff. Use the sketch to identify crossing routes, cramped work areas, and places where dirty utensils or raw ingredients could interfere with ready-to-eat food. Review sanitation, equipment placement, and installation details with the relevant authority.

Give packing its own trial during menu testing. Lay out containers, lids, sauces, labels, and bags, then ask one person to assemble several different orders. Move frequently used items within reach and establish a consistent final check for contents and order identification.

## Confirm requirements and regulations before signing

In the United States, restaurants and other retail food establishments are generally regulated by state and local authorities rather than directly licensed at the federal level. Contact the authority responsible for your proposed address early, explain the menu and operating model, and request its opening checklist. Requirements differ by jurisdiction, so another operator’s approval is not a substitute for your own review.

Ask which registrations, licenses, training, inspections, and approvals apply to your business. Discuss food-service permitting, any plan review, zoning, building work, fire protection, and proposed equipment before making irreversible commitments.

For shared premises, get written clarification of your responsibilities versus the facility operator’s. Ask whether your business needs its own permit and whether additional brands or processes change the approval. Use the answers to establish your opening sequence and avoid promising a launch date before required reviews are understood. ## Prove the economics before increasing order volume

Calculate contribution per order before judging the concept by revenue. For delivery food, build that variable-cost calculation around the costs attributable to each order, using your actual contracts and purchasing figures.

**Contribution per order = net sales − ingredients − packaging − channel fees − other variable order costs.**

For illustration only, assume a $25 order has $8 in ingredients, $1.50 in packaging, $6.25 in commission, and $2 in other variable costs. That leaves $7.25 toward fixed costs and profit. The commission assumption represents 25%; major marketplaces publish tiered delivery plans subject to their terms.

With hypothetical fixed costs of $12,000 monthly, $7.25 contribution requires approximately 1,656 orders per month to break even. This is an example, not an industry benchmark. Include baseline payroll and other fixed expenses consistently, avoid counting costs twice, and use a weighted contribution figure when products or ordering channels differ.

Compare direct ordering with marketplaces using a complete cost worksheet. Ask about payment processing, delivery fulfillment, software, promotions, refunds, and customer acquisition rather than treating commission-free ordering as cost-free. Check current provider terms before setting prices.

## Launch with a controlled operating plan

Start with limited hours and a menu your team has rehearsed. Assign responsibility for preparation, cooking, packing, order acceptance, and customer issues, even when one person covers several roles. Use mock services to establish when extra packing support becomes necessary.

Before choosing ordering technology, request a demonstration using your actual workflow. Test menu changes, modifiers, sold-out items, ticket routing, refunds, and reporting. Decide how staff will handle an internet outage or a failed printer before accepting live orders.

Finish your rehearsal by recording preparation time, missing items, packaging failures, food waste, and contribution by channel. Expand only after the team can repeat the process reliably. The strongest ghost kitchen investment is not simply the cheapest kitchen: it is a controlled commitment supported by a tested menu, verified premises, clear requirements, and workable order economics.
