Restaurant prime cost combines the food and beverages you use with the full cost of employing your team. Tracking it against sales shows how much revenue remains for rent, utilities, other overhead, and profit. For Restaurant Site Finder readers, this guide explains the calculation, puts benchmarks in context, and offers a weekly routine for turning numbers into operating decisions.

## What is prime cost in a restaurant?

Prime cost is the sum of cost of goods sold, or COGS, and fully loaded labor costs for the same period. COGS captures food and beverage consumption; loaded labor includes wages plus employer-paid payroll costs. Together, they connect purchasing, kitchen production, staffing, and sales in one management measure.

The two categories belong together because improving one can increase the other. Buying prepared ingredients might raise food costs but reduce prep hours. Making everything from scratch could reverse that trade-off. Looking at both sides helps you judge the overall result rather than celebrate a saving that simply moves expenses elsewhere.

Prime cost is not your total operating cost, and the percentage remaining afterward is not your profit margin. Rent, utilities, repairs, marketing, and other expenses still need to be paid. A restaurant with an apparently reasonable ratio can therefore remain unprofitable if its other expenses are too high. For margin context, see [restaurant profit margins and unit economics](/blog/restaurant-profit-margins-unit-economics).

## How do you calculate restaurant prime cost accurately?

The prime cost formula is COGS + fully loaded labor cost. To express it as a percentage, divide that total by net sales for the same period and multiply by 100. Accuracy depends on matching dates and including inventory movements and employer labor costs—not just invoices and hourly wages.

### Calculate inventory-based COGS

Use this starting formula:

**COGS = Beginning inventory + Purchases − Ending inventory**

Purchases alone do not show what you consumed. A large delivery can increase this week’s spending while much of the stock remains available for next week. Record credits and transfers consistently, and document waste, employee meals, and promotional use so unusual movements can be explained.

For example, assume these hypothetical weekly figures:

| Inventory component | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Beginning food and beverage inventory | $4,500 |
| Purchases | $6,500 |
| Ending inventory | $5,000 |
| **COGS** | **$6,000** |

Include food and alcoholic and nonalcoholic beverages. Packaging and disposables may also belong in COGS when your accounting policy treats them as direct costs. Keep that policy consistent across periods and locations; otherwise, an apparent improvement may only reflect a classification change.

### Add fully loaded labor

Labor should capture more than the hourly payroll total. Include hourly wages, overtime, salaried restaurant management, employer payroll taxes, benefits, workers’ compensation, and applicable bonuses. Allocate salaries and employer costs to the period being measured, even when payment dates fall elsewhere.

Suppose wages and allocated management salaries total $5,900, while employer taxes, benefits, and related costs add $1,100. Loaded labor is $7,000. Using wages alone would leave that $1,100 out of the calculation, making performance look better without changing the restaurant’s actual expenses.

Use actual employer costs where available. If you need an estimated burden factor for weekly reporting, derive it from your own payroll history and reconcile it later rather than assuming every restaurant has the same benefits and tax structure.

### Divide by matching sales

Continuing the hypothetical example:

| Weekly measure | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| COGS | $6,000 |
| Loaded labor | $7,000 |
| **Prime cost** | **$13,000** |
| Net sales | $22,000 |
| **Prime cost percentage** | **59.1%** |

The calculation is ($13,000 ÷ $22,000) × 100 = 59.1%. That leaves $9,000 before other operating expenses—not $9,000 in profit.

Use a documented net-sales definition consistently, excluding sales tax and handling discounts, refunds, tips, and service charges appropriately. Compare the same operating dates across sales, inventory, and labor reports.

## What is a good prime cost percentage?

Common planning ranges are approximately 55–60% for quick-service restaurants and 60–65% for full-service restaurants. Treat these as starting points, not universal pass-or-fail standards. Your service model, menu, wage structure, sales volume, and remaining expenses determine whether a particular ratio works.

| Restaurant model | Common planning range | What to examine alongside it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quick service | 55–60% | Staffing productivity, packaging treatment, and throughput |
| Full service | 60–65% | Service intensity, menu complexity, and check average |
| Specialized or premium concepts | Establish a concept-specific target | Ingredient mix, preparation requirements, and remaining overhead |

Restaurant365 similarly distinguishes quick-service targets from higher casual-dining ranges. Meanwhile, the National Restaurant Association explicitly cautions that its operating data are management tools, not standards or goals for individual restaurants. A reported industry result and an achievable budget target are different things.

To set your own target, work backward from the business model. In a simplified hypothetical budget, if other operating expenses consume 30% of sales and your desired operating profit is 8%, prime cost must fit within the remaining 62%. Check the dollar assumptions behind those percentages before adopting the target.

Also examine the mix. A hypothetical seafood concept with 38% COGS and 22% labor has the same 60% prime cost as a restaurant running 30% in each category. Neither split alone proves which operation is healthier.

## Build a weekly routine that managers can maintain

Weekly reporting gives managers a chance to respond before a monthly statement arrives. Review sales and labor daily for emerging issues, but distinguish estimates from inventory-verified results. A dashboard updated every hour is not necessarily an accurate hourly COGS calculation.

Consider this repeatable workflow for a busy operation:

1. **Choose a fixed cutoff.** Count at the same point in the operating week, ideally when stock is relatively low and movement is limited.
2. **Arrange count sheets by storage location.** Follow the walk-in, freezer, dry storage, and bar in a predictable sequence rather than searching alphabetically.
3. **Standardize units.** Define whether each item is counted by case, bottle, pound, or portion. Make partial-container methods explicit.
4. **Close the receiving record.** Capture deliveries, supplier credits, and transfers belonging to the week before finalizing the calculation.
5. **Review exceptions before publishing.** Recount implausible quantities and investigate sudden value changes rather than immediately assuming waste or theft.

As a practical check, have a second person verify expensive or frequently miscounted items. If you conduct additional spot counts, keep them separate from the complete weekly inventory calculation. The goal is a dependable routine, not a perfect-looking spreadsheet.

POS, inventory, invoice-processing, and scheduling systems can reduce duplicate entry and connect sales with purchasing and staffing information. Before buying software, identify the specific bottleneck: missing invoices, inconsistent counts, payroll allocation, or slow reporting. Then evaluate whether the tool addresses that problem without adding another disconnected workflow. ## Lower prime cost without weakening the guest experience

Effective restaurant cost control means improving the combined result, not cutting food or staffing indiscriminately. Begin with the category that moved, identify its underlying cause, and test a targeted response. Protect guest satisfaction and staff retention while evaluating whether the change actually improves margins.

### Tighten food cost management at the source

Start with purchasing, recipe quantities, portioning, and waste. Compare current ingredient prices with recipe costs, then investigate the items responsible for the largest dollar changes. Separate price increases from usage problems: renegotiating a case price will not resolve oversized portions or recurring spoilage.

Consider the cost of prime rib in restaurant operations. The invoice price per pound is only the starting point; a useful costing exercise should also account for usable yield, portion size, sides, and unsold portions. Labor remains part of the broader prime-cost calculation rather than disappearing into the meat’s purchase price.

As a hypothetical example, a $180 roast yielding 20 sellable portions has a meat cost of $9 per portion before accompaniments. If it yields only 18, that becomes $10. Testing yield and portion assumptions may therefore be more informative than simply choosing a cheaper supplier.

### Match labor to demand and workload

Use expected sales, ordering patterns, and busy periods to plan coverage rather than copying the previous schedule unchanged. Compare scheduled hours with actual hours, and examine overtime or shifts that repeatedly exceed the plan. Scheduling tools connected to POS data can make those comparisons easier.

Try staggered start times or revised prep assignments before making broad cuts. For each proposed reduction, specify which work will disappear, move, or become more efficient. If the answer is merely that the remaining team will work faster, treat the saving as unproven.

### Evaluate menu changes on combined economics

Review menu items for both ingredient spending and production demands. A dish with attractive food cost may still require substantial prep, while a higher-cost ingredient might simplify execution. Menu engineering should inform purchasing, pricing, and staffing decisions together.

For any proposed change, use a small decision checklist:

- **Calculate the combined effect:** What happens to COGS dollars and loaded labor dollars?
- **Check operational feasibility:** Can you actually remove the saved hours or redirect them productively?
- **Protect the promise:** Will portion quality, availability, or service suffer?
- **Set a review point:** Compare results after a defined trial rather than assuming the forecast was correct.

### Improve sales without mistaking volume for profit

Prime cost percentage also changes when sales change. However, additional sales generally require evaluating additional food, packaging, and staffing costs. Review promotions against their expected operating demands rather than judging success only by order count.

For illustration, $13,000 in prime cost equals 59.1% of $22,000 in sales, but 56.5% of $23,000. That improvement assumes prime-cost dollars stay unchanged. Do not build a growth forecast around that assumption unless existing capacity can realistically absorb the extra business.

## Turn the report into a shared operating habit

Give managers a compact weekly scorecard showing sales, COGS, loaded labor, prime cost, budget, and a comparable prior period. Assign an owner and follow-up action to meaningful variances. Reconcile operational estimates with the monthly accounting close so two competing versions of performance do not develop.

In team discussions, focus on observable behaviors rather than blame: a revised prep quantity, consistent portion tools, or better shift handoffs. Explain the reason for a change and invite practical feedback from the people doing the work. Share aggregate results where useful without exposing individual compensation.

### Make the number useful

The Restaurant Site Finder takeaway is straightforward: calculate consistently, compare thoughtfully, and act while the information is still useful. Start with one reliable week, identify one meaningful variance, and assign one practical improvement. Repeat that process without sacrificing the food and service guests came for.
